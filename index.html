01 — THE BASICS

Understand the threat.

Knowing how social engineering works makes suspicious messages easier to recognize—and safer to handle.

01 / 06 ⌁

What is phishing?

Phishing is a form of social engineering in which an attacker impersonates a trusted person or organization to persuade someone to reveal information, open a harmful file, send money, or take another risky action.

The message is the bait. Your attention is the target.

A COMMON ATTACK FLOW

01
Make contact

A message arrives unexpectedly, appearing to come from someone you trust.

02
Create a reason to act

Urgency, curiosity, fear, or a tempting reward pushes you to respond quickly.

03
Ask for a risky action

Click a link, open an attachment, share a code, or change payment details.

04
Exploit the result

Information or access may be used for account takeover, fraud, or further attacks.

✉

Email phishing

Bulk messages that imitate familiar brands or services.

⌖

Spear phishing

Tailored messages aimed at a specific person or team.

♜

Whaling

Targeted impersonation of senior leaders or executives.

▣

Smishing

Phishing delivered through SMS or messaging apps.

◖

Vishing

Voice calls that pressure people to disclose or do something.

⧉

Clone phishing

A copied message with a link or attachment swapped for a malicious one.

02 — RECOGNIZE THE SIGNS

Look closer before you click.

One clue does not prove a message is fraudulent. Look for mismatches, unexpected requests, and pressure to act outside normal processes.

✉EMAIL

Suspicious messages

  • !Sender mismatchDisplay name says “IT Helpdesk”; the address is an unrelated domain.
  • !Artificial urgencyThreats of immediate account closure or unusual deadlines.
  • !Unexpected attachmentEspecially when you were not expecting a file or invoice.
  • !Odd wording or linksUnusual grammar, unexpected requests, or link text that hides its destination.
⌑LOGIN PAGES

Fake sign-in screens

  • !Lookalike domainExtra words, swapped characters, or an unexpected domain ending.
  • !Unexpected sign-in promptA link in an unsolicited message asks you to re-enter credentials.
  • !HTTPS is not proofEncryption protects a connection; it does not prove a site is trustworthy.
  • !Odd contextThe branding, request, or sign-in flow does not match your usual service.
◎WEBSITES

Untrustworthy destinations

  • !Misspelled URLRead the full domain carefully, from right to left before the path.
  • !Too-good-to-be-true offerPrizes or discounts that require immediate action or sensitive details.
  • !Unexpected redirectsA link takes you somewhere different from what you expected.
  • !Pressure to share dataDo not use a page just because it looks polished or familiar.
SPOT THE MISMATCH

From: Northstar Bank Support security@northstar-alerts.example.invalid — “Your account will be locked in 10 minutes. Verify now.”

Unexpected urgency + unrelated sender domain + sign-in link = pause and verify another way.

03 — THE HUMAN ELEMENT

Understand the pressure tactics.

Attackers often exploit normal human instincts. Recognizing the tactic gives you a moment to slow down and check.

01

Fear & urgency

“A suspicious login was detected—act now or lose access.” The deadline is designed to crowd out careful checks.

02

Authority

“The director needs gift cards before the meeting.” A claimed senior role should not bypass verification.

03

Trust

“Here is the document we discussed.” A familiar name or copied conversation can still carry an unsafe link.

04

Curiosity & greed

“See the confidential announcement” or “claim your surprise prize.” Unexpected rewards can be bait.

05

Impersonation

A caller claiming to be your bank asks you to “confirm” a one-time code. Hang up and call a trusted number.

06

Business email compromise

A message that appears to come from a leader or supplier requests a payment or bank-detail change. Confirm through a known, separate channel.

Ⅱ

Make a pause your superpower. Unexpected request? Stop, verify it through a channel you already trust, and follow your organization’s reporting process.

04 — LESSONS FROM THE FIELD

Real incidents. Lasting lessons.

These summaries distinguish publicly documented incidents from simplified training scenarios. They are not step-by-step attack instructions.

2013–2015DOCUMENTED FACTS

Google & Facebook: invoice fraud

U.S. court records describe a Lithuanian man using spoofed business emails and fraudulent invoices to induce Google and Facebook employees to send more than $100 million to bank accounts he controlled.

Signals & impactSpoofed business correspondence and fraudulent invoices led to more than $100 million in transfers.
LessonIndependently verify supplier changes and large or unusual payment requests. A familiar business relationship is not verification.
U.S. Department of Justice summary ↗
2013DOCUMENTED FACTS

Target: third-party access

Investigations into the 2013 Target breach reported that attackers used credentials stolen from a third-party HVAC vendor to gain an initial foothold before reaching Target’s payment systems. The incident exposed payment-card data and personal information belonging to millions of customers.

SignalsCompromised vendor access and unusual movement from a third-party foothold toward payment systems.
LessonProtect vendor access, use least privilege and MFA where available, and investigate unusual access patterns.

05 — SAFER EVERY DAY

Small habits. Stronger defenses.

Use these practical checks at work and at home. If you make a mistake, report it promptly—fast reporting can help limit harm.

✓

Verify the senderInspect the full address, not just the display name. Confirm unusual requests independently.

✓

Inspect links and destinationsNavigate using a known bookmark or typed address instead of an unexpected link.

✓

Use MFA and unique passwordsPrefer phishing-resistant MFA when available. A password manager helps create and store unique passwords.

✓

Handle files with careDo not open unexpected attachments. Verify with the sender through a separate trusted channel.

✓

Report and updateUse your organization’s reporting process. Keep browsers, devices, and software up to date.

IF YOU CLICKED OR SHARED INFORMATION

Act quickly.
There is no shame in reporting.

  1. Contact your IT or security team using a known channel.
  2. If you entered a password, change it from a trusted device and tell your team.
  3. Follow your organization’s instructions; do not delete evidence or forward the message unless asked.
Prompt reporting helps protect everyone.
